# Interview Playbook

**Who this is for:** Any FDE (Field Delivery Engineer) running a schema-forge engagement — lead or junior.

**What this covers:** How to run the six interview templates, in sequence, so that the outputs are actually useful. This is the soft IP that turns a set of good questions into a good engagement. A junior FDE who follows this playbook can run the engagement as well as the lead; a lead who ignores it will still produce shallow inputs.

---

## The core principle

**You are collecting evidence, not assertions.**

Every question in every template is designed to elicit a specific past event, a named company, a dollar amount, a behavior — not a generalization. "We tend to sell to mid-market SaaS companies" is an assertion. "Our last five closed-won deals were: [company A, 80 employees, Series B fintech], [company B, 140 employees, professional services]..." is evidence.

Evidence produces schema overrides. Assertions produce marketing copy. When a client answers with an assertion, your job is to push through it to the underlying evidence. Every time.

---

## Pre-engagement prep (before Template 01 goes out)

**Read the client's public materials first.** Spend 30–45 minutes on their website, their LinkedIn, and whatever recent content you can find from the founder. The goal is not to become an expert — it is to arrive knowing what they sell, roughly who they sell to, and one or two intelligent questions that signal you did your homework. Clients notice when you have not done this, and it changes how forthcoming they are for the rest of the engagement.

**Identify the right attendees before scheduling any call.** The templates specify required attendees. Do not substitute. If the head of sales is not available for Template 02 (ICP interview), reschedule — not having the sales perspective means the evidence is incomplete. If the founder refuses to attend Template 02, that is information worth noting (it usually means there is a gap between what the founder believes about the ICP and what the sales team actually experiences).

**Send Template 01 at least 48 hours before the first call.** Not 24 — 48. Clients underestimate how long it takes to look up the answers to section 2 (deal economics), and you want the form back with real numbers, not estimates.

**Review Template 01 before Templates 02–04.** The channel ranking in Template 01 Section 5 sets the agenda for Template 03. The deal economics inform what deal-size patterns to look for in Template 02.

## Running the live sessions

### Never lead the witness

The most common FDE mistake is suggesting an answer before the client has given one. "Do you primarily sell to mid-market companies?" is a leading question. "Walk me through who your last five clients were" is not. The difference matters because leading questions produce confirmation bias in both directions — clients will confirm a frame you offer them even if it is wrong, because it is faster than correcting you.

If you are unsure what answer to expect, do not reach for a multiple-choice. Reach for an open prompt: "Walk me through that," "Give me a specific example," "What does that look like in practice?"

### Always ask for specific examples

When a client gives a generalization, ask for the specific example beneath it. This is not confrontational — it is clarifying. A useful framing: "Can you give me a specific deal or client that fits that description?" If they cannot, the generalization is probably not grounded in evidence and should not become an override.

Practice variants of the probe:
- "Walk me through a specific case."
- "What's the most recent example of that?"
- "Which of your last five clients fits that description?"
- "Give me the exact situation."

You will use one of these probes on average every 5–7 minutes in a well-run session. If you are going more than 10 minutes without using one, you are probably accepting assertions.

### Always ask what would surprise an outsider

Once per session — not once per section — ask some version of: "What would an outsider who read your website and your LinkedIn carefully still get wrong about [the topic at hand]?"

This question reliably surfaces the most valuable information in the session. The things clients say here are the things that do not appear in any public taxonomy, any competitor's schema, any generic sales playbook. They are the 30% that makes the schema theirs.

The question works because it gives clients permission to say something that sounds counterintuitive or embarrassing — "our best clients are usually ones who are behind, not ahead" or "the title on the contract is almost always wrong about who actually decided." These are the ICP overrides and policy rules that no amount of firmographic data would surface.

### Timebox aggressively — clients fatigue at 90 minutes

The engagement involves up to four live sessions. Each is designed to run within its stated duration. Do not let any session run long.

The 90-minute ceiling is real. Past 90 minutes of cumulative live time in any given week, client engagement drops measurably — answers get shorter, probes get brushed off, the "outsider question" stops producing good answers. Respecting the timebox is not a constraint on quality; it is the thing that preserves quality across the full engagement.

Tactics:
- **Hard-stop at the scheduled end time.** Thank them, tell them what you got, confirm the next steps. Do not "just do one more thing."
- **If a section is running long, cut the lowest-priority part.** Template 02 (ICP) specifies that if you are short on time, cut Section 4 (the triage exercise) — not the deal walk-through. Every template has this priority note in the FDE instructions.
- **Never double-book sessions in the same week.** Two 60-minute live sessions in one week is the practical maximum. If you need three, schedule the third for the following week.

### Record everything, with permission

Obtain explicit consent before recording any session. A simple verbal confirmation at the start of the call is sufficient: "Just to confirm, you are comfortable with me recording this session for note-taking purposes, with the recording staying within the engagement team?" If they say no, take notes by hand — but make sure to do so.

Recordings serve two purposes: (1) you can quote the client's exact language in the override rationale fields, which makes the audit trail stronger; (2) you can review unclear moments after the call without going back to the client for clarification.

Do not rely on recordings as a substitute for post-call notes. Post-call notes should be written within 30 minutes of the session, before the context fades. The recording is a backup and a reference, not the primary capture mechanism.

## Handling difficult clients

### The abstract generalist

Some clients are accustomed to describing their business in marketing language and will resist moving to specifics. Signs: every answer contains the word "typically," every example is unnamed, every size is "it depends."

Counter-tactic: go to the won-deal walk-through (Template 02, Section 1) immediately and do not leave it until you have at least three named or described specific deals. The specificity required by that section breaks the generalist pattern for most clients, because they cannot walk through a specific deal without giving specifics.

### The oversharer

Some clients, especially founders, will give you 10 minutes of context for every question. You will run out of time.

Counter-tactic: interrupt politely and early. "That's really helpful context — let me stop you there and ask you to put that on the back burner. I want to make sure we get through [section]. Can you give me the short version of [the specific question] and we'll come back to the context?" If you let the first 20-minute answer go uninterrupted, you have signaled that the pace is acceptable.

### The deflector

Some clients, especially in larger firms, will redirect uncomfortable questions ("what deals do you regret taking?") to generalities or to criticism of competitors. They are not lying — they are protecting the relationship.

Counter-tactic: normalize the question before asking it. "Before I ask this, I want to say that every firm we have worked with has had a version of this — it's not a judgment, it's how we calibrate the failure-mode library. Walk me through a deal you would not take again."

### The client who says they don't have gold records

Some clients will push back on Template 06 — "our CRM is a mess," "we don't track it that way," "our notes are in email."

Counter-tactic: lower the threshold explicitly. "Fifty records is the goal. If you can give me twenty with real notes, that is still worth running. Even a Google Sheet you maintain by hand is fine. Can you commit to 20 records in the next 48 hours?" Most clients can produce 20 records; the pushback on 50–100 is about the perceived effort, not the actual data availability.

If they genuinely cannot produce any records, make a note in the engagement: calibration will be generic until the client provides examples. Do not pretend you have a client-specific calibration when you do not.

## Session sequence and dependencies

The templates are designed to be run in order. The dependencies are:

1. **Template 01 must be returned before any live session.** It sets the baseline that Templates 02–04 build on.
2. **Template 02 before Template 03.** The channel ranking in Template 03 should be informed by which channels the won deals came from (Template 02, Section 1 "channel this came from" fields).
3. **Template 02 before Template 04.** The evidence gate questions in Template 04 are easier to ask against the backdrop of specific deals already discussed in Template 02.
4. **Template 05 can run in any order after Template 02.** The regrets from Template 02 and the failure modes from Template 05 are distinct captures — regrets are deal-level, failure modes are signal-level.
5. **Template 06 can run in parallel with Templates 03–05.** Start the gold-records ask early — it takes clients longer to pull than they expect.

Optimal scheduling for a typical engagement:
- **Week 1:** Send Template 01 Monday; receive by Wednesday; Templates 02 and 03 Thursday/Friday (or Thursday and following Monday).
- **Week 2:** Template 04 Monday or Tuesday; Template 05 async by Wednesday; Template 06 export received by end of week.
- **Week 2–3:** FDE runs `extract-overrides`, reviews overrides with the client in a 30-minute check-in, runs `generate`.

Total calendar time: 2–3 weeks. Total client time: ~3 hours live + 1 hour async.

## What to do if a session goes wrong

### You forgot to ask the outsider question

Go back async. Send a single-question follow-up: "One thing we did not get to in our session: what is the most counterintuitive thing about your ICP that someone who read your website would still get wrong? Can you answer that in a paragraph?" Most clients will answer; the written answer is often as useful as the spoken one.

### The answers are too thin to produce overrides

If a session produced answers that are all generalities and no specifics — every deal is unnamed, every failure is vague — do not move forward to `extract-overrides` immediately. Schedule a 20-minute follow-up specifically for the won-deal walk-through (Template 02, Section 1). Tell the client explicitly: "The output we produce is only as specific as the inputs we have. I need three specific deals with the details we discussed." Most clients will engage when the connection between specifics and output is made clear.

### The gold records never arrive

After 72 hours of no response to Template 06: send a single follow-up with the threshold-lowered version ("20 records is fine if 50 is hard"). After a week of no response: flag the engagement as uncalibrated. Do not block the rest of the engagement — run `generate` with the generic calibration and document in `manifest.yaml` that `gold_records` was not provided. The output is still useful; it is just not client-specific on calibration.

## Quality check before `extract-overrides`

Before running `extract-overrides`, do a manual review of all six templates against this checklist:

**Template 02 (ICP):**
- [ ] At least 4 of 5 won deals have company size, industry, title of signer, and deal-trigger filled in
- [ ] At least 3 of 5 lost deals have a specific loss reason (not "went with a competitor")
- [ ] At least 2 of 3 regrets have a specific missed pre-sale signal
- [ ] The outsider question produced at least one non-obvious constraint

**Template 03 (Channel):**
- [ ] At least 2 channels ranked with qualitative notes (not just a number)
- [ ] At least one "good signal" described with a specific behavior (not just "they seem interested")
- [ ] The warm-prospect definition has a specific timeframe and at least one observable behavior

**Template 04 (Qualification):**
- [ ] Evidence gates filled in for at least 2 of 3 stages (pre-discovery, pre-proposal, pre-contract)
- [ ] Primary deal-breaker is specific (not just "budget" but how budget manifests as a deal-killer)

**Template 05 (Failure modes):**
- [ ] At least 2 of 3 false positives have a specific false signal described
- [ ] The fake-yes pattern has at least one behavioral marker the client can name

**Template 06 (Gold records):**
- [ ] At least 20 records received with disposition and raw notes
- [ ] No more than 20% mismatch between raw notes and disposition (spot-check passes)

If three or more items on this checklist are missing, schedule a follow-up before running `extract-overrides`. The LLM normalization step can clean up rough language and fill in minor gaps — it cannot invent evidence that was never collected.

---

## A note on judgment

This playbook is a set of defaults, not a set of rules. The questions are designed to surface evidence. If a client gives you evidence through a different path — an unexpected story, an unprompted example, a spontaneous confession about a regret — follow it. The templates are scaffolding. The evidence is what matters.

When in doubt: more specific is always better than less specific, and a follow-up question is always better than accepting a vague answer.
