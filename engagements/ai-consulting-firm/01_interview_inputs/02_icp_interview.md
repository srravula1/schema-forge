## Section 3 — Deals you regret taking

**Regret R-1**

- Client descriptor: Seed-stage AI startup (document processing), 12 employees, San Francisco
- What went wrong post-sale? We scoped a RAG sprint but discovered 3 weeks in that their data was entirely unstructured PDFs with no metadata, no clean corpus, and no ground truth labels. We essentially had to do data preparation work that was outside our scope to deliver anything. Finished 4 weeks late, over budget internally, and the client was unhappy because the timeline slipped even though we delivered.
- What was the pre-sale signal you missed or rationalized away? They said "we have all our documents in S3." We didn't probe what "all our documents" meant or what state they were in. The red flag was that they couldn't give us a sample corpus during scoping — they said "we'll get you access when we start." We accepted that.
- If you saw the same pre-sale signal again, what would you do differently? Require a sample of 50 representative documents before scoping. If they can't provide samples, we can't scope accurately. This is now a hard requirement in our discovery process.

**Regret R-2**

- Client descriptor: VC-backed AI consulting firm (our direct competitor in AI governance advisory), 25 employees
- What went wrong post-sale? The client had an internal team that disagreed with hiring us — specifically, their senior ML engineer wanted to build the eval system himself. The project became a politics problem from day 1. The internal champion (their CEO) was overriding his team to work with us, which meant every deliverable got second-guessed and every decision required a politics meeting. We finished the project but the relationship ended badly.
- What was the pre-sale signal you missed or rationalized away? During discovery, the champion (CEO) mentioned offhandedly that "the team has mixed feelings about this." We asked one follow-up question, got a reassuring answer, and moved on. We should have required to meet the skeptical team members before signing.
- If you saw the same pre-sale signal again, what would you do differently? Require a conversation with the skeptical internal stakeholder. If the internal team isn't aligned on working with an external firm, no amount of CEO buy-in fixes it. This is now a mandatory pre-contract step.

**Regret R-3**

- Client descriptor: PE-backed professional services firm (management consulting), 200 employees
- What went wrong post-sale? The project scope was clear but the client's internal "AI project owner" changed 3 times during the engagement. Each new owner had different priorities, requested scope changes, and didn't have full context on decisions the previous owner had made. We delivered technically but the product never got deployed because no one internally owned it.
- What was the pre-sale signal you missed or rationalized away? The client couldn't tell us who the internal owner of the project would be during discovery. We asked "who will be our day-to-day contact?" and they said "we'll figure that out when we start." We accepted it because the deal size was attractive ($140k).
- If you saw the same pre-sale signal again, what would you do differently? Require an internal project owner — with a name and title — before signing. This person must have 20% of their time committed to the project. No named internal owner = no deal, regardless of size.

## Section 4 — Triage exercise

**FDE prompt:** "Imagine I gave you a list of 1,000 companies..."

> _Notes:_ "First filter is size — under 20 employees goes in the never bucket unless they're a specific type of company (like an AI-native startup with $5M+ in funding and a technical founder). Over 500 employees goes into a different bucket — we can sometimes do a small audit, but a sprint is unlikely to clear their procurement process in under 6 months. The sweet spot is 30–200 employees. Second filter is funding or cash flow — bootstrapped with revenue or Series A+ for sprint-size deals. Pre-Series A seed companies almost never have the project readiness or budget. Third: is there a named internal AI/engineering owner? If the company doesn't have someone who can own AI projects, our work won't get deployed. I'd rather the company have a Head of Data or Head of Engineering who's already thinking about AI than have a CEO who just decided they need AI because they saw it at a conference."

**Follow-up — the "never" bucket specifically:**

> _Notes:_ "'Never' is: pre-seed or seed stage companies with under 20 employees, or companies where the engagement with us came from someone's personal AI curiosity rather than a business problem. If I get a DM that says 'I loved your post on RAG, I want to explore what AI could do for us' with no specific project in mind — that's a never, at least for now. The word 'explore' is a red flag. Real buyers say 'we have X problem and we need to solve it by Y date.' The explorers say 'I want to see what's possible.'"

**Follow-up — timing signals:**

> _Notes:_ "'Call this week' is triggered by specificity and urgency — they've said something like 'we're trying to ship this by end of Q2,' or 'our board is asking about AI and we need something to show them,' or 'we just had an engineering hire fall through and we need to move.' Any of those create a call this week. Moving from 'nurture' to 'active' happens when they start asking implementation-specific questions — not 'can AI do X' but 'how would you handle X edge case in our specific setup.' That's a buying question, not an exploring question."

## Section 5 — The outsider question

> _Answer 1:_ "The biggest misconception is that our bottleneck is budget. It's not. We lose more deals to 'no project ready' than to 'no budget.' Someone can have $150k approved and still not be ready — because they have no data, no internal owner, no success criteria. We've learned to test project readiness before we ever ask about budget. Budget is table stakes; project readiness is the deal-breaker."

> _Answer 2:_ "LinkedIn engagement is not a buying signal. It's an interest signal. The people who comment and share our posts are almost never buyers. The buyers are lurkers who read everything and then DM us with a very specific, technical question about a problem they're facing right now. The specificity of the question is the real signal — 'can AI help?' is an explorer; 'we're using GPT-4 for document extraction and getting 40% hallucination rate on financial data, what's your approach to this?' is a buyer."

> _Answer 3 (if they surface it):_ "Series A is not the right filter either — it's 'do they have cash available for a non-headcount spend?' Some Series A companies have all their funding allocated to headcount and can't free up $75k for a vendor. Some well-run bootstrapped companies have plenty of cash and are much better clients. The real filter is: can they write a $50k+ check without a 3-month internal approval process?"

## FDE post-call notes

**Patterns across won deals (firmographics, timing, channels):**

> 5 won deals: 40–120 employees (median ~85), all post-Series A or profitable bootstrapped. 3 from LinkedIn inbound, 1 from VC referral, 1 from personal referral. All had a named internal technical owner (CTO or Head of Data equivalent). All could articulate a specific problem and a success criterion. Average deal $95k.

**Patterns across lost deals:**

> Lost deals where we competed: lost on team size (L-1, company 300 employees), lost to pricing pressure from a cheaper option (L-3, developer tools). Lost due to external events (L-2, CTO departure; L-5, budget timing). One deliberate disqualification (L-4). The pattern: our real competitors are "hire someone internally" and "go with a bigger firm for credibility."

**Disqualification signals that surfaced (from regrets section):**

> 1. No named internal project owner — regret R-3, now a hard requirement
> 2. "We'll figure that out when we start" as an answer to any scope question — regret R-1 (data access) and R-3 (owner identity)
> 3. Internal team skepticism / "mixed feelings" — regret R-2
> 4. Seed-stage companies with raw, unstructured data and no data engineer — regret R-1

**Most surprising / counterintuitive ICP constraint:**

> Funding stage is a proxy, not the real filter. The real filter is "can they write a $50k+ check and do they have a project-ready problem." Some bootstrapped companies clear this; some Series A companies don't. The engagement should test for this directly rather than using funding stage as a categorical proxy.
