## Section 2 — Evidence gates at each stage

### Before a discovery call

> _Required evidence:_ Company size (between 20 and 200 employees), a named technical lead (CTO, VP Eng, or equivalent), and a stated problem or use case — even if vague. "We're trying to do X with AI" is enough to get a discovery call. "We want to explore AI" is not.

> _What happens if it is missing?_ If there's no named technical lead, we ask to be connected to one before scheduling. If there's no stated problem, we send them a piece of content and ask them to come back when they have a specific use case in mind. We don't run discovery calls purely for relationship-building.

### Before sending a proposal

> _Required evidence:_ (1) Need articulated — they've described the problem in enough specificity that we can scope a solution. (2) Authority confirmed — we've spoken to or been confirmed to know who signs. (3) Data access confirmed — we've reviewed a sample corpus or confirmed they can provide one during scoping. (4) Timeline stated or discoverable — they have a target, even if soft. Missing any of these, we go back to discovery.

> _What happens if it is missing?_ No proposal. We tell the prospect explicitly what we need before we can propose. If they can't provide the sample data or can't name a decision-maker, we treat it as a project-readiness gap, not a budget gap.

### Before sending a contract

> _Required evidence:_ (1) Signed scope document or clear verbal scope confirmation. (2) Internal project owner named — we require a named person with 20% of their time committed. (3) Payment terms confirmed (50/50, specific dates). (4) Data access plan confirmed (not just promised — an actual setup call scheduled).

> _What happens if it is missing?_ We hold the contract until the owner is named and the data access plan is confirmed. We've learned the hard way (regret R-3) that starting without a named owner creates an unmanageable engagement.

## Section 3 — The deal-breaker

> _Primary deal-breaker:_ "No named internal project owner. Not 'who will be our contact,' but 'who owns this project post-delivery and has time allocated to work with us.' If they can't name that person, we don't sign — doesn't matter how good the budget is or how aligned the champion is."

> _Follow-up — has this changed in the last year?_ "This was always true; we just didn't enforce it. After the PE-backed consulting firm disaster (regret R-3), we made it an explicit gate. It has prevented at least 2 bad engagements since then."

> _Secondary deal-breaker:_ "Data readiness. If they can't provide a sample corpus during scoping — even 50 documents — we can't scope accurately and the engagement will have cost overruns. The data situation determines everything about timeline and price."

## Section 4 — AI consulting specific

> _Answer — rank or describe:_ "Timing kills more deals than anything else — but by 'timing' I mean internal project readiness, not calendar timing. The project isn't real yet is the dominant kill. Budget is almost never the issue for companies that have made it to our discovery call — they self-select by reaching out to a $75k+ engagement firm. Authority kills deals maybe 10% of the time, usually when the champion isn't actually the decision-maker and the real decision-maker is skeptical. But timing / readiness accounts for at least 50% of our stalls and losses."

**Follow-up — timing specifically:**

> _Notes:_ "A real-but-early project looks like this: they have a specific use case, they can name the data they'd use, they have a target outcome, but they're 3–6 months from internal approval. We can work with that — we nurture. A project that will never happen looks like this: they're enthusiastic, they have no specific use case, they can't name the data they'd use, and when you press them on what success looks like they give a vague answer. The tell is 'we want to explore what's possible' — real buyers know what they want to achieve, they just don't know how."

**Follow-up — internal readiness:**

> _Notes:_ "Yes, there's a readiness check we do. Three components: (1) Data readiness — can they provide a sample? (2) Owner readiness — is there a person allocated to this? (3) Infrastructure readiness — do they have the minimum internal infra to deploy something (at minimum: an engineer who can read Python and a cloud account)? If any of those three are missing, the engagement will have problems regardless of how well we execute. We call this the 'three readiness gates' internally."
