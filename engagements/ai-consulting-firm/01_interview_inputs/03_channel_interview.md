## Section 2 — What a good signal looks like per channel

**Channel C-1: Founder LinkedIn**

- What does a good inbound signal look like? "A DM or email that contains a specific, technical, business-contextualized question. Not 'can AI help with X?' but 'we're using Claude 3 for document extraction and getting too many hallucinations on financial data — have you dealt with this and what's your approach?' The presence of a vendor name, a specific failure mode, and a business context in the first message is almost always a buyer."
- What does a bad or fake signal look like in this channel? "Someone who comments on multiple posts enthusiastically, follows, and sends a DM that says 'I love your content, I'd love to chat sometime about what AI could do for my business.' Warm and engaged but not a buyer. Also: 'Can you tell me more about what you do?' — anyone who doesn't know what we do after reading the profile and posts is not a serious buyer yet."
- How long after a good signal do you typically reach out? "If they DM me, same day. I try to respond within 4 hours to a business-specific question — if they're a buyer, they're often evaluating multiple options and speed signals quality."
- Who on your team owns this channel? Founder directly.

**Channel C-2: Warm referrals**

- What does a good inbound signal look like? "An intro from someone who has worked with us — 'I used them at my last company for X, you should talk to them about Y.' The intro email itself tells me a lot — if the referrer took time to write a specific intro describing the match, the prospect is serious. If it's 'you two should meet' with no context, treat it like a warm cold outreach."
- What does a bad or fake signal look like in this channel? "A generic 'you two should meet' intro where the referrer doesn't specify why. These are often 'favor introductions' where the referrer is just clearing their inbox. Still worth following up, but lower conviction."
- How long after a good signal do you typically reach out? "Same day for a specific intro. Within 48 hours for a generic one."
- Who on your team owns this channel? Founder + head of sales, split.

**Channel C-3: VC / partner referrals**

- What does a good inbound signal look like? "A portfolio company intro from an investor who knows our work — this usually comes with specific context ('they need to ship AI infrastructure by end of Q2 and I told them you're the right team'). When a VC is specific about the need, it's because they've diagnosed it and we're their answer."
- What does a bad or fake signal look like in this channel? "VC intros where the VC is just doing 'helpful investor' favors — connecting everyone in their portfolio to everyone they know. These often produce long qualification conversations that go nowhere because the company wasn't actually looking for what we sell."
- How long after a good signal do you typically reach out? "Within hours for a warm VC intro — VCs notice response time."
- Who on your team owns this channel? Founder.

## Section 3 — Channels that have not worked

**Channel that failed — F-1**

- Channel: Cold outbound (email sequences)
- What did you try specifically? Built a list of ~500 companies from LinkedIn using filters (Series A/B, tech companies, 50–200 employees). Ran a 4-email sequence over 3 weeks. Personalized the first email with LinkedIn research.
- How long / how much did you invest before stopping? 3 months, ~$8k in tools and contractor time.
- What was the failure mechanism? Very low reply rate (~0.8%), and the replies we got were mostly polite declines. One deal ever, which was a $25k audit that didn't expand. The problem: the people we were emailing were not in "project ready" mode — they were being targeted because they fit the profile, not because they had a pressing problem.
- In retrospect, why did it fail? "AI consulting is a pull business, not a push business. People hire us when they have a problem they've already identified and can't solve internally. Cold email reaches them before that problem is acute, so we're either too early or irrelevant. Our value proposition requires the prospect to already have a felt need."

**Channel that failed — F-2**

- Channel: LinkedIn Sales Navigator outbound (connection requests + DMs)
- What did you try specifically? Targeted CTOs and VPs of Engineering at Series A/B SaaS companies with 50–150 employees. Sent connection requests with a short intro message referencing their company or recent activity.
- How long / how much did you invest before stopping? 6 weeks, founder did it directly (~5 hours/week).
- What was the failure mechanism? Accepted connection rate was fine (~30%), but reply rate on the follow-up DM was very low. When people did reply, the conversation almost never progressed to a call. The founder felt the channel was "polluting" their LinkedIn network — people they wanted as community members, not people they'd tried to sell to.
- In retrospect, why did it fail? "The audience I'm trying to reach — CTOs who care about AI quality — are sophisticated LinkedIn users who are bombarded with exactly this kind of outreach. My thought leadership posts are what give me credibility with them; an outbound DM undercuts that credibility. It's a positioning conflict."

**Channel that failed — F-3**

- Channel: Content syndication (guest posts on AI/ML publications)
- What did you try specifically? Wrote 4 long-form pieces for Towards Data Science, The AI Exchange, and one VC firm's blog. Each took 6–10 hours to write.
- How long / how much did you invest before stopping? 4 months, ~40 hours of writing time.
- What was the failure mechanism? Traffic but no leads. Readers were primarily ML practitioners and students, not buyers. No DMs, no deal attribution.
- In retrospect, why did it fail? "Wrong audience. The people who read 'how to build a RAG system' tutorials are building, not buying. Our buyers are CTOs and VPs who want to understand what's possible at a strategic level, not engineers who want implementation details. The LinkedIn posts work because they're written for decision-makers; the long-form tutorials reach practitioners."

## Section 4 — LinkedIn deep-dive

> _Answer:_ "100% founder posts, organic. No sponsored content (we've never run LinkedIn ads). No Sales Nav outbound in any meaningful way — I tried it and stopped, as described above. Partner referrals sometimes happen through LinkedIn but the relationship itself came from in-person or newsletter contact. The LinkedIn mechanism is: I post a take on something in the LLM / AI engineering space 3–5 times per week — a mistake I see companies making, a framework for evaluating RAG systems, a real benchmark comparison, a lesson from a recent engagement. People follow because they find it useful. The buyers self-select when they have a real problem."

**Follow-up — the mechanism in detail:**

> _Notes:_ "The last deal that started on LinkedIn: the VP of Engineering at a Boston-based data infrastructure company (Deal W-4). He had followed me for 6 months. The specific trigger was a post I wrote about RAG evaluation benchmarks for structured data — he commented with a technical question about retrieval recall. I answered in the comments and then DM'd him to continue the conversation. He said 'actually, this is exactly the problem we're working on — can we hop on a call?' That was the start of an $140k engagement. The move from LinkedIn to a real conversation always starts with them asking a problem-specific question, not me pitching."

**Follow-up — engagement patterns:**

> _Notes:_ "Signals that make me prioritize an outreach: (1) they've commented on 3+ posts in the last 30 days — not just liked, but commented with substance; (2) the comments are problem-specific, not generic ('great post'); (3) they've DM'd with a question before and I haven't yet responded substantively. When I see someone who has been silently following and then asks a specific question, I treat that as a warm outreach opportunity — they've already done their research."

## Section 5 — Warm-prospect definition

> _First answer:_ "30 days before they're ready to buy, the signal is: they've started asking specific implementation questions in a context that implies they already have the project internally. Not 'can AI do X' — 'we're trying to do X, here's our current setup, what's your approach?' That shift from hypothetical to operational is the tell."

> _Probe — what would you literally look at to confirm?_ "I'd look at whether they've engaged with multiple posts on related topics — not just one. A one-time comment might be curiosity. A pattern of comments across related posts (RAG + evaluation + deployment, for example) suggests they're actively thinking through a specific architecture decision. I'd also look at whether they've shared or re-posted something of mine — that's a stronger signal than a comment because they're putting their name on it to their network."

> _Second signal (if they surface one):_ "Hiring signals — if a company is actively hiring for 'AI Engineer' or 'ML Platform Engineer' roles, they have internal momentum on an AI project that probably needs help. That's a warm outreach trigger even without LinkedIn engagement."

**Follow-up — timing triggers:**

> _Notes:_ "Triggers 30–90 days before a deal: (1) they've just closed a funding round and want to put it to use; (2) a CTO or Head of Engineering has just joined or was promoted — new technical leaders often want to make an AI investment as a signal of their agenda; (3) they've had an AI project fail internally and are now open to external help; (4) their competitors have launched an AI feature and the pressure is on. Of these, (3) and (4) are the most reliable — internal failure and competitive pressure create urgency that funding alone doesn't."
